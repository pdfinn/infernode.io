Today we start a new fork of Inferno®. Before writing about what we are building, I want to say where it really begins, because it begins more than twenty-six years ago.

## A laptop, an early Darwin, and one thing that worked

Around the turn of the century I had a G3 Apple laptop running one of the very first releases of Darwin, the open-source core of what became Mac OS X. Those were early days. In the first few months, very little built on Darwin without a lot of work. Ports needed patching, build systems assumed other Unixes, and getting even a basic graphical environment up was a project of its own.

One of the first things I got running on it was Inferno®, under its emulator. What I remember most is how surprised I was. It built cleanly and it ran, on a brand-new platform that almost nothing else supported yet. In a few minutes I had a whole operating system running inside another one, with its own shell, its own window system and its own programs, and it all just worked.

I was hooked from that day. I have wanted other people to have that experience ever since.

## Where Inferno® came from

Inferno® came from the Computing Sciences Research Center at Bell Labs, the group that created Unix and then Plan 9. Plan 9 took Unix's best idea, that everything is a file, and applied it everywhere. Devices, network connections and the window system are all files, served over one protocol, 9P. Every process has its own namespace, a private view of that tree assembled from whatever has been bound into it.

Inferno® took those ideas and made them portable. It was designed for small, networked devices such as set-top boxes and phones. It could run directly on hardware, or hosted as an ordinary program on Windows, Linux and other Unixes. Its programs are written in Limbo, a concurrent language whose channels and processes will look familiar to Go programmers, for good reason. Limbo compiles to bytecode for the Dis virtual machine, so the same compiled program runs anywhere the system does. The names come from Dante: Inferno, Limbo, Dis, the Styx protocol, and the Charon web browser.

It moved from Bell Labs and Lucent to Vita Nuova, which released later editions and eventually the source. In 2021 it was relicensed under the MIT licence. By then most of the world had moved on, and the code had not kept up with the machines.

## What an operating system can be

What hooked me was not nostalgia. Inferno® is a reminder of what an operating system can be.

It is small. The emulator, the Dis virtual machine, the Limbo compiler and the core libraries together come to about 200,000 lines of code. Everything else, including the shell, the window system, the editors, the network services and the applications, is written in Limbo on top of that. It is small enough for one person to read and to understand how the pieces connect. The headless emulator binary is about a megabyte. It runs comfortably in memory that a modern browser tab would consider a rounding error.

It is also clean. There is one way to name things, which is the file tree. There is one way to talk to things, which is 9P. There is one way to restrict things, which is the namespace. Very little is special-cased, and a design that small and consistent is easy to trust.

You do not need tens of millions of lines of code to have a real operating system. Inferno® proved that in the 1990s, and the point has only become more important since.

## Why now

Twenty-six years later, I think the moment for those ideas has arrived. We are starting to give software agents real access to our machines, and the question of what a program can reach has become urgent. Inferno®'s answer, a namespace containing only what has been granted, is the cleanest one I know.

Using that answer requires a modern Inferno®: 64-bit, fast, current on today's hardware, with today's cryptography. That is a big gap to close, and closing it is the project we start today. The [next post](/blog/why-infernode/) describes how, and what we want to build on top of it.

I hope that somewhere along the way, someone builds InferNode for the first time, watches it come up, and is as surprised as I was.
